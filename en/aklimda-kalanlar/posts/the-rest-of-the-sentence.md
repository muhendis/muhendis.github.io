Imagine a friend stirring their tea and saying, “Lately, I just don’t want to go home.” Before the spoon even hits the saucer, several answers come to mind. Maybe a change of scenery would do them good. Maybe they just need to talk through whatever is bothering them at home. Right at that moment, our friend continues:

“Whenever I open the door, I still feel like calling out to my mother.”

Hearing that second sentence makes us realize how little we understood from the first. When someone we care about hurts, our instinct is to act. Having a practical suggestion ready gives us a sense of relief. In our rush for that relief, how often do we cut someone off mid-thought?

In his essay *Why Do They Listen to Deniz?*, Doğan Cüceloğlu shares a letter from Kezban Küçük recounting a family gathering. When young Deniz joined the adults’ conversation, they paused and listened attentively. The other children couldn’t understand Deniz’s German. What Küçük remembered decades later was the adults’ patience in letting Deniz finish his thought.

At work, too, hearing one more sentence often completely changes the decision we make. In a meeting, when someone says, “This won't be ready by Friday,” our immediate reaction is often, “Let’s add another person.” Perhaps the task is simply larger than expected. Or perhaps the team has been waiting since Tuesday for sign-off from another department. In the first scenario, extra help makes sense. In the second, picking up the phone and calling the right stakeholder is all that’s needed. Asking, “Where are we stuck right now?” helps us understand what is truly needed.

In an experiment conducted by Harry Weger and colleagues with strangers meeting for the first time, participants who were actively listened to reported feeling significantly more understood. Their overall conversational satisfaction was comparable to those who received explicit advice. When a friend asks, “Can you come with me tomorrow?”, responding with “What time?” is often enough. Sometimes people state their needs plainly. Other times, they discover it for themselves as they speak.

What if the tea cools down a little? Is it really necessary to rush just because we have an answer ready? Perhaps what our friend actually needs to say is in the rest of the sentence.

> “To speak well and truthfully, I practice patience, gratitude, prayer, listening—and sometimes, simply silence.”  
> — **Şenol Güneş**
